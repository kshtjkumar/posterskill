Network level shifts in E/I balance during chronic epilepsy revealed by aperiodic EEG dynamics

Abstract:

Chronic seizures progressively reshape brain-wide connectivity and excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance, but medication effects make these dynamics difficult to study in patients. Using a chronic epileptic Lafora disease (Epm2a−/−) mouse model, we tracked how network-level E/I balance evolves across epileptogenesis and ictogenesis using the aperiodic exponent, a noninvasive EEG proxy for E/I ratio and beta-band oscillatory metrics. Laforin deficient (Epm2a−/−) Lafora disease mice exhibited steeper baseline aperiodic slopes and elevated beta activity, indicating increased tonic inhibition. Isoflurane, a GABA-A receptor agonist, produced stronger spectral slope steepening in wild-type animals than in knockouts, reflecting altered GABAergic tone in Epm2a−/− mice. During chemically induced seizures, aperiodic measures showed systematic variation across pre-ictal, ictal, and post-ictal periods, revealing phase-specific shifts between inhibitory and excitatory dominance.These results suggest that cortical E/I balance varies dynamically across seizure phases and severity. By resolving these transitions in real time, aperiodic EEG features offer a promising biomarker for seizure staging and vulnerability assessment. This work provides mechanistic insight into network-level adaptations in chronic epilepsy while avoiding pharmacological confounds.

Keywords: Excitation/Inhibition ratio, noninvasive EEG markers, spectral parameterization, aperiodic slope, periodic oscillation, seizure, chronic epilepsy

1.1  Introduction 

Epilepsy is a dynamic network disorder in which recurrent seizures progressively reshape brain-wide structure, connectivity, and the balance between excitation and inhibition (E/I). Repeated seizures trigger long-lasting neuroplastic changes (Wang et al., 2025), destabilize local and global functional organization (Barrios-Martinez et al., 2025), and increase vulnerability to subsequent events (Liang et al., 2020). Disruptions in E/I balance are considered a central hallmark of epileptogenesis (Liu et al., 2023), reflected in alterations in the Hurst exponent in chronic temporal lobe epilepsy (Xie et al., 2024), dynamic E/I fluctuations across seizure phases (Žiburkus et al., 2013), and seizure-induced strengthening of excitatory synapses via BDNF signalling (Nasrallah et al., 2022). However, how E/I balance evolves across seizure stages and grades remains poorly understood, particularly in humans, where ethical constraints and anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs) obscure intrinsic circuit dynamics.

Although epilepsy is often framed as a disorder of impaired inhibition, several lines of evidence challenge this canonical view. Absence seizures exhibit stronger inhibitory tone and reduced E/I ratios (Eisenstein et al., 2024), and autosomal dominant nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy features inhibitory synchronization via nicotine-evoked enhancement of GABAergic output (Klaassen et al., 2006; Mann & Mody, 2008). Such findings support a dynamic rather than static role of E/I imbalance in ictogenesis and epileptogenesis (Shao et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2024). At the cellular level, seizure initiation, propagation, and termination are shaped by oscillatory shifts in inhibitory and excitatory drive (Žiburkus et al., 2013), yet how these processes manifest at the systems level in vivo remains unclear.

The paradoxical role of inhibition in ictogenesis is further underscored by evidence that maladaptive GABAergic signaling, arising from receptor plasticity, genetic alterations, or pharmacological interventions, can destabilize inhibitory homeostasis and predispose to seizures (Shao et al., 2019; Krueger-Burg, 2025). Tonic and phasic inhibition may exert distinct influences on seizure onset and termination (Belete, 2023; Lőrincz et al., 2024), but clinical EEG studies are limited by AED effects and insufficient temporal resolution to resolve fast E/I transitions.

Aperiodic EEG activity, once regarded as nonspecific neural noise, is now increasingly recognized as a physiologically meaningful signal and has emerged as a candidate biomarker across multiple neurological and psychiatric disorders (Phillips et al., 2024; Woronko et al., 2025, Aggarwal & Ray.,  2025). Recent conceptual advances highlight the potential of the aperiodic (1/f) structure of the neural power spectrum as a network level proxy for E/I balance. Computational models link spectral slope in the 30–50 Hz range to shifts in excitation and inhibition (Gao et al., 2017; Chini et al., 2022). Empirical studies show that the aperiodic slope steepens under inhibitory conditions such as sleep or anesthesia (Lendner et al., 2020; Saberi et al., 2025) and flattens during heightened excitation (Höhn et al., 2024). A complementary spectral offset parameter provides insight into broadband excitability (Donoghue et al., 2020). Although steepening of the aperiodic slope has been reported during ictal states (Liu et al., 2023) and in temporal lobe epilepsy (Duma et al., 2024), its relationship to seizure phase, severity, and evolving E/I balance remains unresolved.

Pharmaco-EEG and MEG studies suggest that changes in canonical frequency bands may partly reflect alterations in the aperiodic component (Donoghue et al., 2020; Ostlund et al., 2022), which is modulated by GABA-A receptor activity (Muthukumaraswamy et al., 2013; Stock et al., 2020). Meanwhile, GABA-A receptor subtype plasticity (Fritschy, 2008; Mathew et al., 2012) and inhibitory network dynamics shaping beta oscillations (Yamawaki et al., 2008; Rossiter et al., 2014) further highlight the need for analytical approaches that jointly capture periodic and aperiodic features when probing cortical E/I balance.

Here we combine a chronic epilepsy knockout model with pharmacological modulation and spectral parameterization to map E/I dynamics across seizure phases and grades. Using bilateral screw-EEG recordings in wild-type and Laforin knockout (Epm2a−/−) mice, we examine whether aperiodic EEG parameters track tonic and phasic inhibitory processes at baseline, during GABA-A receptor modulation, and across seizures of varying intensity. This framework enables assessment of intrinsic E/I dynamics without AED confounds and evaluates the translational potential of aperiodic EEG features as biomarkers of seizure vulnerability and severity.

2. Methods/Methodology

2.1 Experimental Animals

Laforin-deficient mice represent a validated animal model of Lafora disease, a fatal progressive myoclonus epilepsy characterised by abnormal glycogen accumulation and neurodegeneration (Parihar and Ganesh., 2024). The Epm2a−/− mouse model of Lafora disease was generated as described previously (Ganesh et al., 2002). The terms laforin knockout (LKO) and Epm2a−/− are used interchangeably throughout the manuscript. All experiments were performed in accordance with the institutional animal ethics committee (IAEC) guidelines after obtaining approvals for the protocols (IAEC project number: IITK/IAEC/2021/1137).Mice were bred in the CEAF (Central Experimental Animal Facility), at IIT Kanpur. Both male and female mice were used for the study. Animals were group-housed in standard cages under conventional laboratory conditions and individually housed from the day of electrode implantation onward. Environmental conditions were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C temperature, 55 ± 5% humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7 a.m.). Food and water were provided ad libitum.

2.2  Surgical Protocol, Animal Preparation

All surgical procedures were conducted on both wildtype and transgenic C57BL/6 mice (Laforin knockout model) using aseptic techniques. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% for induction, 1.5% for maintenance in 100% O₂) and secured in a stereotaxic frame ( Stoelting Co.). Anesthetic depth was periodically monitored by confirming the absence of a pedal withdrawal reflex. Body temperature was maintained at 37°C throughout the procedure using a feedback-controlled heating pad. For local analgesia, a subcutaneous injection of lidocaine (0.1 mg/kg) was administered at the incision site. A midline incision was made along the scalp to expose the skull. The skull surface was cleaned, and the stereotaxic landmarks of Bregma and Lambda were clearly identified. Five burr holes were drilled through the skull using a 0.8 mm micro-drill bit, taking care not to penetrate the underlying dura mater. A 1 mm long titanium screw was threaded into each burr hole until it made firm contact with the dural surface.

Four active screw EEG electrodes were implanted stereotactically over the frontal and temporal cortices, with ground and reference electrodes placed over the cerebellum. Coordinates relative to Bregma were: right frontal (AP +2.5 mm, ML +1.3 mm), left frontal (AP +2.5 mm, ML −1.3 mm), right temporal (AP −3.0 mm, ML +2.5 mm), left temporal (AP −3.0 mm, ML −2.5 mm), and reference (AP −6.0 mm, ML 0.0 mm). The lead wires from the screws were soldered to a multi-pin connector, which was then securely fixed to the skull. All implanted electrodes were fixed to the skull with dental glass ionomer cement. The sockets were attached to a wire electrode, and the assembly was secured to the skull by silicon glue. This formed a durable head cap for subsequent recordings. Post-surgery, animals were individually housed and monitored for pain and distress.Animals were allowed a recovery period of 3-5 days before the commencement of electrophysiological recordings.

2.3 EEG Acquisition

For post-surgical pain management, animals received meloxicam (5 mg/kg; Boehringer Ingelheim, GA, USA).

Electrophysiological recordings were carried out in freely moving mice using the Intan RHD2000 series recording system (Intan Technologies, LLC). Animals were connected via a lightweight and flexible tether to minimize movement restriction. All recordings were performed inside a Faraday cage to reduce environmental electrical noise and prevent signal loss during transmission.

Neuronal signals were acquired using the Intan RHS stim/recording system (Intan Technologies) in combination with an RHS32 low-noise amplifier headstage. The data were digitized at a sampling rate of 30 kHz, resampled 2kHz. To suppress power-line interference, a 50 Hz notch filter was applied for online real-time visualization. In addition, real-time band-pass and line-noise filtering were employed to improve the ability to assess neuronal activity during recordings and to assist in determining appropriate probe insertion depth. These filters were used exclusively for online monitoring and did not affect the stored raw data.

During acquisition, a hardware reference was applied to further minimize common-mode noise and improve signal quality across all channels.

2.4 PTZ dose:

PTZ is an epileptogenic compound used to induce epilepsy and measure neuronal excitability.For the induction of seizures, a single dose of PTZ 50 mg/kg (convulsive) (Sigma-Aldrich™, St. Louis, U.S.A.; purity ≥ 99%) was administered intraperitoneally.

2.5 Seizure grading:

Seizure severity in PTZ-induced mice was evaluated using a modified Racine scale (Van Erum et al., 2019). This scoring system quantifies seizure-related behavioral manifestations, with each stage corresponding to a progressive increase in seizure severity. Scores 0–2 were categorized as partial (focal) seizures and classified as low-grade events. Scores 3–4 represented generalized seizures, characterized by bilateral motor involvement and loss of postural stability classified as high-grade events. Higher scores (5–7) denoted severe tonic–clonic seizures, marked by sustained clonus, tonic posturing, and potential loss of righting reflex. Scoring was performed by trained observers blinded to experimental groups to ensure reliability and reproducibility.

2.6 EEG Data Processing and Analysis

All offline data processing and analysis were initiated in Brainstorm software (Tadel et al., 2011), running in MATLAB (version R2024a; The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). The continuous raw data from the four cortical electrodes were imported into Brainstorm and re-referenced by subtracting the signal from the cerebellar reference electrode. The data were then filtered using a 1–100 Hz band-pass filter and a 50 Hz notch filter to remove line noise. Following preprocessing, the entire recording was visually inspected by an expert reviewer to identify and mark electrographic seizures.

For quantitative analysis, the continuous data from both baseline and post-PTZ events were segmented into non-overlapping 30-second epochs. Epochs containing significant artifacts or electrographic seizure activity were excluded to ensure that subsequent spectral analysis reflected underlying, non-epileptic brain states.

To decompose the power spectral density (PSD) of clean epochs into periodic and aperiodic components, we used the 'Fitting Oscillations & One-Over-F' (FOOOF) algorithm (Donoghue et al., 2020), implemented in Python. This separation is crucial as traditional band-power measures can conflate these two signals (Donoghue et al., 2020). The FOOOF algorithm models the aperiodic component as a 1/f function and identifies periodic components as Gaussian peaks rising above the aperiodic slope.

Power spectral densities were estimated using Welch’s method with a Hamming window, as implemented in the Brainstorm toolbox. The FOOOF algorithm was applied to the PSD of each epoch, modeling the spectrum over a frequency range of 1–30 Hz. The algorithm parameters were set as follows: peak width limits = [1.0, 8.0] Hz; maximum number of peaks = 6; minimum peak height = 0.1; peak threshold = 2.0 standard deviations; and aperiodic mode = 'fixed'. From the model output for each epoch, we extracted the aperiodic parameters (offset and exponent) and the periodic parameters (center frequency, power, and bandwidth of each identified oscillatory peak). These extracted parameters were then used for all subsequent statistical comparisons and classification analyses.

To estimate the excitation–inhibition (E:I) balance across screw EEG electrodes, we computed the 1/f power slope using the FOOOF algorithm. The aperiodic exponent (slope) of the power spectrum has been proposed as a proxy for cortical E:I balance, with computational models and empirical evidence demonstrating that flatter slopes (lower exponents) indicate relatively higher excitation, while steeper slopes (higher exponents) reflect relatively higher inhibition (Gao et al., 2017). Both the aperiodic parameters (exponent and offset) and the band-specific oscillatory powers were then used for all subsequent statistical comparisons.

2.6.1 Data Quality Control

Prior to statistical analysis, data quality was ensured through two filtering steps. First, only epochs with FOOOF model fits achieving an R² > 0.8 were retained, ensuring high-quality spectral parameterization. Second, to remove outliers in the aperiodic exponent, we excluded epochs where the exponent deviated by more than 2 standard deviations from the animal-specific mean, calculated separately for each animal.

2.6.2 Data Visualization

For aperiodic parameters (exponent and offset), data were visualized using box plots with notches representing the 95% confidence interval around the median. A yellow diamond-shaped marker indicated the mean value for each condition.

For band-specific oscillatory parameters, center frequencies (CF) were visualized using Gaussian density curves. For each experimental stage, the mean center frequency and mean bandwidth (STD) across all detected peaks within that band were calculated. These summary statistics were used to generate normal distribution curves with the mean CF as the peak location and the mean bandwidth as the spread. Vertical dotted lines marked the mean CF for each stage. This visualization approach represented the average spectral characteristics of oscillatory activity within each frequency band while accounting for inter-epoch variability.

Oscillatory power (amplitude extracted from FOOOF) were visualized using box plots with the same format as the aperiodic parameters notches representing the 95% confidence interval around the median and a yellow diamond-shaped marker indicating the mean value for each condition.

2.6.3 Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Bayesian multilevel regression models implemented in the brms package (Bürkner, 2017) in R (version 4.x.x; R Core Team, 2024). Bayesian approaches were chosen for their ability to account for hierarchical data structures, provide full posterior distributions for parameter estimates, and quantify uncertainty in a probabilistic framework (Kruschke, 2014).

All models included random intercepts for animal_ID to account for inter-animal variability. Models were fit using 4 chains with 2000 iterations each (1000 warmup), and convergence was assessed via R-hat values (all < 1.01). A fixed random seed (123) was used to ensure reproducibility. The specific model structures used for different analyses (see Model List). In general, models included experimental conditions as fixed effects with appropriate interaction terms, following the general form:

Outcome ~ Predictor(s) + (1 | animal_ID)

2.6.4 Effect Size Calculation

For all models, effect sizes were calculated as standardized coefficients by dividing each Stage coefficient (β_Stage) by the residual standard deviation (σ) from the model:Cohen's d = β_Stage / σ. This approach yields standardized effect sizes representing the magnitude of stage differences relative to within-unit residual variation, independent of measurement scale. These standardized coefficients were summarized using posterior means and 95% credible intervals.

2.6.5 Model Validation

Posterior predictive checks were conducted for all models using the pp_check() function from BRMS to assess model fit by comparing observed data to data simulated from the posterior predictive distribution. This ensured that the models adequately captured the data-generating process.

3. Results:

3.1 Enhanced global cortical inhibition in the epileptic Epm2a−/− mouse

Understanding how changes in excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance shape seizure vulnerability requires separating aperiodic and oscillatory contributions to cortical activity. Although the causal links between E/I shifts, disease progression, and behavior are complex, disruptions in homeostatic synaptic plasticity can alter excitatory and inhibitory synapses in parallel or in distinct phases, ultimately influencing circuit function at multiple scales (Chen et al., 2022). In Epm2a−/− mice, previous work has documented a reduction in cortical GABAergic neurons, indicating impaired tonic inhibition and altered inhibitory tone (Ortolano et al., 2014). To assess how these cellular changes manifest at the systems level, we analyzed resting-state wake EEG using spectral parameterization to dissociate aperiodic and periodic components and compare them across Laforin knockout (LKO) and wild-type (WT) animals. Based on the reported loss of GABAergic neurons in neonatal LKO mice, we initially expected reduced inhibition and greater excitatory drive, reflected in flatter aperiodic slopes and elevated beta activity.

We first examined aperiodic parameters during wakeful rest. Subject-specific aperiodic fits were derived from individually estimated exponent and offset values, with representative WT and LKO traces illustrating higher epileptiform discharge rates in knockout mice (Fig. 1A–B; stats not provided). Contrary to our initial hypothesis, Epm2a−/− animals showed significantly higher aperiodic exponents (1.35 ± 0.31) relative to WT mice (1.10 ± 0.23) (Fig. 1C; n = 12, and 48 sessions (30-min each); β = 0.31, 95% CI [0.09, 0.53]; effect size = 1.57, 95% CI [0.47, 2.69]). This steepening indicates elevated baseline inhibitory tone in LKO mice. Aperiodic offset did not differ significantly between groups, though LKO animals showed a numerical increase (2.24 ± 0.95 vs. WT: 1.86 ± 0.47; Fig. 1D). Reconstructed aperiodic components in log–log space further highlighted this group-level steepening in LKO mice (Fig. 1F). These patterns were consistent across bilateral frontal and temporal channels, suggesting a global increase in cortical inhibition.

We next examined oscillatory (periodic) beta activity while controlling for aperiodic contributions. Subject-specific beta peaks were reconstructed using the individual central frequency for each animal, benchmarked against the canonical WT beta range (centered at ~19 Hz; 16–30 Hz). Epm2a−/− mice showed a shift towards higher beta central frequency (Fig. 1G; n = 48, β = 3.18, 95% CI [1.01, 1.17]; effect size = 1.11, 95% CI [0.35, 0.41]). Beta power, adjusted for aperiodic components, was also significantly higher in LKO animals (Fig. 1H; n = 48, β = 0.17, 95% CI [0.07, 0.28]; effect size = 0.92, 95% CI [0.36, 1.5]). Additionally, aperiodic-adjusted delta power was elevated in LKO mice (Supplementary Fig:1), consistent with reports of cognitive impairment in this model.

Together, these findings reveal a coordinated shift in both aperiodic and periodic EEG features. Steeper aperiodic slopes, along with increased beta frequency and power, point toward enhanced tonic inhibitory tone in Epm2a−/− mice at baseline potentially reflecting a widespread cortical adaptation to persistent epileptiform activity. Elevated delta activity further aligns with known cognitive deficits in this model. Overall, these results support the interpretation that the aperiodic exponent serves as a sensitive systems-level marker of global cortical inhibition and may index tonic inhibitory processes that shape seizure susceptibility.

3.2 Isoflurane induced inhibition is compromised in Epm2a−/−  mice

Altered or immature GABAergic signaling is a well-established contributor to heightened seizure susceptibility, and seizures themselves can further disrupt GABA-A receptor function by modifying receptor subunits and altering inhibitory currents (Briggs and Galanopoulou., 2011). Although GABA-A receptor–acting anesthetics typically produce a steepening of the spectral slope, some modulators such as ketamine show inconsistent effects (Salvatore et al., 2024). Here we asked whether genotypic differences in tonic inhibitory tone shape the brain’s responsiveness to phasic GABAergic modulation, quantified through EEG-derived spectral features. Isoflurane was administered during bilateral 4-channel screw EEG recordings (Fig. 2A).

In WT animals, isoflurane produced the expected broadband slowing of the EEG (1–30 Hz) and significantly steepened the PSD, reflected as higher aperiodic exponent (Fig. 2B; n = 8 animals, 32 sessions; β = 0.60, 95% CI [0.09, 1.13]; effect size = 3.04). Importantly, the aperiodic offset remained unchanged (Fig. 2C; n = 8, 32 sessions), indicating that slope steepening reflected a redistribution of spectral power toward lower frequencies rather than a global power shift (Fig. 2H). Isoflurane also increased the aperiodic-adjusted beta central frequency (Fig. 2E; n = 8, 32 sessions; β = 3.11, 95% CI [1.49, 4.71]; effect size = 1.67) and elevated beta power, with this effect being more pronounced in the temporal cortex (Fig. 2F; n = 8, 32 sessions; β = 0.109, 95% CI [0.059, 0.159]; effect size = 0.787).

We next examined how Epm2a−/− mice responded to the same isoflurane concentration (1.5%). Representative EEG traces before and after induction are shown in Fig. 2D–E. Compared to WT, LKO animals displayed markedly different trends of both aperiodic and periodic components. Isoflurane decreased the aperiodic exponent compared to baseline in LKO mice (Fig. 2F; n = 8 animals, 32 sessions; β = −0.06, 95% CI [−0.09, −0.02]; effect size = −0.26), and the magnitude of change induced by IF in WT was tenfold greater than that observed in LKO animals. This altered responsiveness suggests an elevated baseline inhibitory tone in LKO mice, layered on top of previously reported compensatory inhibitory adaptations. Isoflurane also significantly decreased the aperiodic offset (Fig. 2G; n = 8 animals, 32 sessions; β = −0.15, 95% CI [−0.23, −0.08]; effect size = −0.32), indicating an overall reduction in broadband power after anesthesia. A pronounced steepening of the aperiodic slope was evident in LKO animals relative to their baseline (Fig. 2K).

After adjusting for aperiodic structure, LKO animals showed a significant increase in beta oscillatory frequency but a true reduction in beta power (Fig. 2L–M; n = 8 animals, 32 sessions; β = −0.06, 95% CI [−0.08, −0.04]; effect size = −0.51). These combined changes suggest that both tonic inhibitory differences and network-specific oscillatory mechanisms shape how the epileptic brain responds to isoflurane.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the aperiodic slope robustly tracks GABA-A receptor–mediated cortical inhibition, consistent with prior observations that GABA-A receptor–modulating anesthetics steepen the PSD through enhanced phasic inhibition and greater neuronal synchrony (Lendner et al., 2020; Muthukumaraswamy & Liley, 2018). The steeper slopes observed in WT mice compared to LKO animals, along with distinct beta-band signatures, suggest that Epm2a−/− mice with already elevated tonic inhibition respond differently to additional phasic GABAergic engagement. These spectral markers therefore appear sensitive to both genotype and pharmacological state, offering a promising framework for dissecting the mechanisms of cortical inhibition in health and disease.

3.3 Phasic increase in cortical inhibition in the pre- and post- ictal periods

Neuronal network function depends on a tightly regulated balance between excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) inputs. Although seizures are traditionally linked to hyperexcitability, emerging cellular-level evidence shows that the E/I ratio does not rise uniformly; instead, it fluctuates dynamically across ictogenesis, with seizure initiation often driven by increased inhibition. To capture these evolving network states at the systems level, we again used aperiodic spectral parameters as proxies for E/I balance during pharmacologically induced seizures. Tonic–clonic (TC) seizures were triggered using the GABA-A receptor antagonist pentylenetetrazol (PTZ), enabling us to characterize preictal, ictal, and postictal epochs through detailed, epoch-by-epoch spectral parameterization. We hypothesized that cortical excitation would not simply escalate at seizure onset but would transition through distinct inhibitory and excitatory phases.

The aperiodic exponent reflecting the 1/f spectral slope varied systematically across seizure phases. Preictally, exponent values became higher relative to baseline (n = 8, 24 sessions; β = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.18]; effect size = 0.24, 95% CI [−0.20, 0.66]), indicating a steeper slope and dominance of inhibitory tone across frontal and temporal cortices. During the ictal (TC) phase, the exponent flattened markedly compared to preictal levels (n = 8, 24 sessions; β = −0.13, 95% CI [−0.21, −0.04]; effect size = −0.66, 95% CI [−1.09, −0.22]), shifting toward lower exponent  values consistent with heightened excitation and an elevated E/I ratio. Following seizure termination, exponent values became the highest of all three states (n = 8, 24 sessions; β = 0.18, 95% CI [0.06, 0.30]; effect size = 0.66, 95% CI [0.22, 1.21]), reflecting a strong rebound in inhibitory tone a signature of postictal suppression.

The aperiodic offset, indexing broadband power, also showed phase-specific modulation. During TC seizures, the offset increased significantly relative to preictal levels (n = 8, 24 sessions; β = 0.43, 95% CI [0.18, 0.66]; effect size = 0.84, 95% CI [0.35, 1.29]), consistent with a surge in global cortical activation. Postictally, the offset dropped below preictal activity (n = 8, 24 sessions; β = −0.97, 95% CI [−1.22, −0.74]; effect size = −1.89, 95% CI [−2.38, −1.38]), aligning with the inhibitory rebound captured by the exponent.

Periodic features also tracked seizure-related state changes. Beta-band (12–30 Hz) power increased significantly during seizures (n = 8; β = 0.31, 95% CI [0.09, 0.53]; effect size = 1.57, 95% CI [0.47, 2.69]), consistent with cortical desynchronization and heightened excitability. Elevations in aperiodic adjusted power in the delta and sigma bands observed during TC compared to baseline (Supplementary Fig: 2), suggesting broader reorganization of low- and mid-frequency rhythms during ictogenesis.

Together, these results reveal a phase-specific progression in cortical E/I balance: an inhibitory-dominant preictal period, a transition to pronounced excitation during the ictal phase, and a strong inhibitory rebound during postictal suppression. These findings further indicate that in Epm2a−/− mice with already elevated tonic inhibition the dynamics of inhibitory buildup and release may influence seizure susceptibility. Overall, the aperiodic exponent seems to be a sensitive biomarker for tracking rapid, state-dependent E/I fluctuations across seizure phases.

3.4 Increase in excitatory drive during ictal epochs is consistent with seizure grade

Our earlier analyses revealed inhibition-dominated epochs during tonic–clonic (TC) seizures. We next examined whether lower-intensity seizures particularly those lacking a clear preictal stage are driven primarily by inhibition or excitation, and whether aperiodic parameters can reliably differentiate seizure severity. To address this, we analyzed EEG epochs across multiple seizure grades. Based on behavioral features, we hypothesized that grade-2 seizures, which frequently include absence-like spike–wave discharges, would be more inhibition-dominated, whereas grade-4 seizures, characterized by stronger motor involvement, would reflect heightened excitation.

The aperiodic exponent was sensitive to seizure grade, offering a continuous, noninvasive marker of cortical excitability (Fig. 4). Representative traces from LKO animals illustrate grade-2 seizures marked by spike–wave discharges and polyspikes, and grade-4 seizures characterized by higher-amplitude ictal spikes (Fig. 4A).

During grade-2 seizures, the aperiodic exponent significantly decreased relative to baseline (Fig. 4C; n = 8, 32 sessions; β = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.11, −0.06]; effect size = −0.35, 95% CI [−0.46, −0.25]), indicating a flatter slope and a shift toward increased cortical excitation. To test whether these spectral shifts scale with seizure intensity, we next examined grade-4 seizures. In these higher-grade epochs, the aperiodic exponent decreased even further (Fig. 4D; n = 4, 26 sessions; β = −0.20, 95% CI [−0.30, −0.10]; effect size = −0.67, 95% CI [−1.02, −0.32]), reflecting a more intense excitation-dominated state than in grade-2 seizures.

Aperiodic offset values also scaled with seizure intensity. During grade-2 seizures, the offset increased significantly relative to baseline (Fig. 4E; n = 8, 32 sessions; β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.11, 0.21]; effect size = 0.33, 95% CI [0.05, 0.23]), indicating greater overall spectral power. Although grade-4 seizures also showed an increase in offset, this effect did not reach significance (Fig. 4F). Interestingly, in grade-4 seizures, temporal regions showed a significantly lower offset than frontal cortex (Supplementary Fig. 3; n = 4, 26 sessions; β = −0.43, 95% CI [−0.54, −0.31]; effect size = −0.012, 95% CI [−0.64, 0.32]), suggesting region-specific differences in high-grade ictal recruitment.

Consistent with these aperiodic effects, aperiodic slope flattening was evident in both seizure grades, with grade-4 seizures exhibiting the most pronounced flattening (Fig. 4G–H), further supporting increased excitatory drive.

Together, these results show that both grade-2 and grade-4 seizures are marked by flatter aperiodic slopes, with progressively lower exponents and elevated offsets as seizure intensity increases. These spectral signatures reflect a shift toward excitatory dominance and demonstrate that the aperiodic exponent is a sensitive, temporally resolved marker of seizure severity.

4. Discussion

Chronic epilepsy involves progressive, brain-wide alterations in network organization and excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance, yet clinical recordings are frequently confounded by anti-epileptic medications that obscure intrinsic homeostatic adaptations. Using a chronic epileptic knockout model, we examined drug-free network dynamics and show that the aperiodic exponent of the EEG power spectrum provides a sensitive, temporally resolved network level proxy of cortical excitability across seizure stages and severities. By pharmacologically manipulating GABA-A receptor activity and applying spectral parameterization, we tracked changes in E/I balance as the animal transitioned through preictal, ictal, and postictal states, as well as seizure grades.These observations suggest that network-level features associated with E/I balance in epilepsy vary across brain states rather than reflecting a uniform or static alteration, and that such variation can be quantified using aperiodic EEG measures.

Baseline recordings revealed steeper aperiodic slopes in Epm2a−/− mice, indicating elevated tonic inhibition. Although these mice show early-life reductions in cortical GABAergic neurons (Ortolano et al., 2014), tonic inhibition can remain preserved or even increase in epilepsy (Cope et al., 2009; Müller et al., 2020). Importantly, excessive tonic inhibition may paradoxically increase seizure vulnerability by narrowing the dynamic range of inhibitory control and creating a brittle network prone to collapse during excitatory perturbations (Mathew et al., 2012; Mann & Mody, 2008). This interpretation aligns with molecular evidence for altered inhibitory homeostasis in Epm2a−/− mice (Ganesh et al., 2005) and human MEG findings showing elevated slopes during interictal episodes (Kopf et al., 2024).Our findings extend this framework by linking baseline inhibitory tone to cortical spectral organization in a chronic epilepsy model.

Isoflurane anesthesia produced robust slope steepening consistent with enhanced phasic inhibition (Brown et al., 2010; Purdon et al., 2015), with distinct genotype-dependent patterns. Wild-type animals showed pronounced increases in beta power and frequency, whereas Epm2a−/− mice displayed altered modulation, likely reflecting disrupted GABA-A receptor signaling or interneuron recruitment (Bieda et al., 2009; Jensen et al., 2005). Because beta synchronization arises from coordinated interneuronal activity, these findings highlight how tonic and phasic inhibitory pathways are perhaps differentially altered in epilepsy.Importantly, this pharmacological sensitivity strengthens the mechanistic relevance of aperiodic dynamics as a functional indicator of inhibitory tone at the network level.

During PTZ-induced seizures, the aperiodic exponent tracked the evolving E/I landscape: steepening during preictal inhibition, flattening as excitation dominated the ictal phase, and steepening again during postictal suppression. These patterns support a two-step model of ictogenesis in which inhibition first increases to stabilize the network, followed by a transition to sustained excitation that drives seizure propagation (Žiburkus et al., 2013; Rich et al., 2020). The exponent also scaled with seizure grade, flattening more strongly during high-intensity seizures and enabling fine-grained differentiation of seizure severity that extends beyond behavioral scoring alone.Together, these observations demonstrate that aperiodic dynamics capture fine-grained, state-dependent changes in network excitability across seizure evolution.

While the aperiodic exponent has been proposed as a proxy for excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance, it does not represent a direct measurement of synaptic currents. Rather, it reflects population-level spectral organization that emerges from the interaction of excitatory and inhibitory processes. Accordingly, we interpret changes in the aperiodic exponent in a relative, state-dependent manner within animals and across seizure phases, rather than as an absolute estimate of synaptic ratios. That said, our findings provide converging systems-level evidence that the aperiodic exponent can track evolving E/I balance in a chronic epilepsy model. By combining pharmacological manipulations with epoch-wise spectral characterization, we show that tonic inhibition, phasic inhibition, and excitation each leave distinct signatures in the aperiodic slope across seizure phases and grades. These observations build on prior reports of slope changes in human epilepsy and extend them by demonstrating temporally resolved, grade-specific patterns in a genetic model of chronic seizures. Importantly, the convergence of aperiodic dynamics with pharmacological modulation of GABAergic tone and phase-specific seizure progression strengthens the mechanistic relevance of this measure. While preliminary, this framework offers a promising basis for understanding how baseline inhibitory tone may influence seizure vulnerability and how dynamic E/I transitions unfold during ictogenesis.

Our analyses relied on low-channel EEG and a single genetic model of chronic epilepsy, constraining spatial resolution and the generalizability of absolute quantitative values. Future work applying this framework to high-density EEG, intracranial recordings, and human datasets will be essential for testing its generality across species and disease contexts. In addition, PTZ-induced seizures provide controlled access to pre-ictal, ictal, and post-ictal states but do not capture the full heterogeneity of spontaneous epilepsy.

In summary, this work demonstrates that excitation–inhibition balance in chronic epilepsy is dynamically reorganized across seizure evolution and severity, rather than statically perturbed. By establishing aperiodic EEG activity as a scalable, state-sensitive descriptor of large-scale network dynamics, our findings provide a principled framework for studying pathological brain state transitions. This approach bridges cellular mechanisms, systems-level electrophysiology, and behavior, and offers a foundation for translational studies aimed at tracking disease progression and therapeutic modulation across neurological disorders.
